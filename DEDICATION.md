### To the Pioneers of Life-Saving Safety Architecture

I also honor four pioneers whose work shows what safety engineering can mean when it is grounded in physical reality and made usable at scale.

* **Sir George Cayley** laid foundational principles of aeronautics by separating lift, propulsion, and control, and by demonstrating human-carrying gliders. His work represents the early transition from intuition to engineered flight—and the corresponding responsibility to protect the human being within the machine.
* **Edward J. Claghorn** received U.S. Patent No. 312,085 in 1885 for improvements in safety belts intended to secure people working or traveling at height and in other hazardous settings. His design embodied a durable principle: safety begins by physically constraining an otherwise dangerous movement.
* **Hugh DeHaven** turned his survival of a 1917 aircraft collision into a systematic study of crash injury and survivability. His research helped establish modern crash biomechanics and showed that survivability depends not only on the force of an accident, but on how structures and restraints manage the body's motion and impact.
* **Nils Bohlin**, a Volvo engineer, developed the modern V-type three-point safety belt introduced in 1959. Volvo then made the design available to other vehicle manufacturers without licensing barriers, allowing a life-saving solution to spread far beyond a single company.

Their work is honored here not because mechanical restraint and software governance are the same invention, but because they share an engineering ethic: identify the failure mode, constrain the dangerous transition, verify the protective mechanism, and make safety practical.

### To Satoshi Nakamoto

I also dedicate this work, in recognition of its intellectual lineage, to Satoshi Nakamoto.

In 2008, a concise paper described a peer-to-peer system for electronic cash and showed how cryptographic proof could reduce dependence on trusted intermediaries. That principle—replace unexamined authority with verifiable rules—forms part of the intellectual bedrock on which LIMEX and Pax Silicea stand.

Where Bitcoin gave digital value a permissionless ledger, LIMEX and Pax Silicea pursue a complementary task: binding machine proposals to evidence, authority, and deterministic execution before they can affect the physical world. This is not a claim to complete Satoshi’s vision. It is an effort to honor its engineering discipline: verify rather than assume, minimize intermediaries, and fail closed whenever authorization or evidence is insufficient.

> *The ledger remains unbent. The execution remains unyielding.*
